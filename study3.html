Study 3 of 4

From research findings to MVP definition

Translating accumulated insights into a prioritized product scope — and learning that defining what not to build is harder than defining what to build

MethodsOpportunity mapping · Workshops · Prioritization
InputsEcosystem research · Agent behavior findings · SME discussions · Business goals
ToolsMiro · FigJam · Excel · Jira

The problem that emerged

By this point in the project, research had done its job — surfacing patterns, challenging assumptions, and generating a genuine understanding of how agents and admins worked. The challenge was no longer discovery. It was decisions.

Across the ecosystem research, behavioral study, workshops, SME discussions, and operational conversations, opportunities had accumulated across nearly every part of the product:

Trust mechanisms Compare & quotation workflows Recommendation systems Dashboards Personalization AI-assisted search Communication layers Loyalty ecosystems Operational tools Search enhancements Workflow continuity

Each idea had emerged from real evidence. Each seemed valuable in isolation. And that was precisely the problem.

"The challenge was not discovering ideas. It was deciding which ideas actually belonged in the first version of the product — and which ones would make it harder to ship anything useful."

Research questions

  • 1Which opportunities appear repeatedly across multiple research inputs?
  • 2Which create meaningful user value versus which just sound compelling?
  • 3What belongs in MVP versus later phases?
  • 4Which ideas increase complexity without proportionate value?
  • 5How do research findings translate into decisions teams can actually act on?

Approach

This study wasn't a single research activity — it was a continuous synthesis exercise running alongside ongoing design and development work. Rather than one prioritization session, it involved repeatedly returning to accumulated evidence as new questions emerged in workshops and cross-functional discussions.

Finding 1

More opportunities didn't automatically create more value

As opportunities accumulated, the instinct was to retain as many as possible — each one had evidence behind it, each had been raised by someone with legitimate knowledge of the problem. Removing anything felt like losing something real.

But as workshop discussions evolved, a different pattern emerged. Ideas that looked valuable individually were creating collective problems: increasing implementation dependencies, expanding scope unpredictably, and making it harder for teams to align on what to actually build first. Loyalty ecosystems, AI-assisted search, and broader personalization concepts each sounded compelling on their own. Together, they were pulling the product in too many directions simultaneously.

The problem wasn't a shortage of good ideas. It was that good ideas collectively create complexity — and complexity is what kills first versions of products.

Finding 2

Users needed fewer, stronger decision signals — not more information

The behavioral research had already established that agents relied on a small decision framework — location, price, reviews — and struggled with trust rather than a lack of options. That finding kept resurfacing in prioritization discussions.

Ideas like more filters, richer property details, and expanded recommendation labels all seemed like reasonable responses to agent frustration. But when mapped against the actual pattern — agents optimizing for speed and certainty, not exploration — a different picture emerged. Adding more information would increase cognitive load at exactly the moment agents needed to move quickly.

Key insight

The question shifted from "how do we provide more information?" to "how do we help users decide faster?" That reframe changed which features made the cut.

Finding 3

External workflows needed integration, not replacement

Research had consistently shown that agents had built their own infrastructure around the platform — WhatsApp for sharing options, email for quotes, spreadsheets for tracking. The initial instinct in some discussions was to build features that would replace these behaviors entirely.

But looking at what those tools were actually doing — enabling sharing, maintaining continuity, supporting client communication — it became clear that replacing them wasn't the right goal. The goal was reducing the fragmentation that made them necessary in the first place. A compare and quotation workflow that let agents share options directly from the platform addressed the same need, without requiring agents to abandon communication patterns that were already working.

Reflection

I came into this expecting prioritization to feel like selection — picking the best ideas from a list. It turned out to be something different: a process of understanding tradeoffs well enough to make decisions that multiple teams could commit to.

The most difficult discussions weren't about whether an idea was good. They were about whether a good idea belonged in this version — and those discussions required the research to be specific and translatable, not just directionally correct.

What surprised me most was how much of the value came from defining what not to build. Removing AI-assisted search and loyalty ecosystems from MVP scope wasn't a loss — it was what made the rest of the scope coherent.

Research gradually stopped being about discovering opportunities and became a process of helping teams decide. That shift — from surfacing possibilities to reducing ambiguity — is where I found the work most meaningful.
