Study 2 of 4

Understanding users at scale

Surveying 124 travel agents and conducting in-depth sessions with internal admin users — to move from ecosystem understanding to behavioral understanding

MethodsSurvey · In-depth interviews · Walkthrough sessions
Participants124 travel agents (survey) · 3–5 GRNconnect admins (interviews)
ToolsTypeform · Teams · Excel

Why this study was needed

Study 1 established the shape of the ecosystem — who the actors were, how work moved between them, and where the structural gaps were. But ecosystem understanding and behavioral understanding are different things. Knowing that agents and admins existed within the system didn't tell us how they actually made decisions, what constrained them, or what they were really optimizing for day to day.

Several assumptions had already taken root on the team — about agents needing more filters, about apartment booking resembling hotel booking, about admin workflows being straightforward to support. These weren't unreasonable. But designing around untested assumptions means optimizing for what teams believe users need, not what users actually do.

This study ran two parallel investigations: a large-scale survey of travel agents to validate behavioral assumptions at breadth, and in-depth interview and walkthrough sessions with GRNconnect admins to understand the operational layer that ApartWorld's infrastructure would need to support.

"Ecosystem understanding told us who was in the system. Behavioral understanding told us what they were actually trying to do — and where the product was making that harder."

Research questions

  • 1How do travel agents actually make decisions under real working conditions?
  • 2Which early assumptions about agent behavior hold up — and which don't?
  • 3How does apartment booking differ from hotel booking in practice?
  • 4What does admin work actually look like day to day — and how much of it happens outside the product?
  • 5What operational infrastructure does ApartWorld need to support from the start?

Approach

Two methods, two user groups — chosen to address different research needs simultaneously.

Travel agents — survey

124 responses via Typeform across agent profile, booking behavior, search and filters, quote sharing, competitor usage, reporting needs, and open-ended frustrations. Quantitative findings analyzed alongside thematic coding of open-ended responses.

GRNconnect admins — interviews & walkthroughs

In-depth sessions with 3–5 admins covering roles and hierarchy, day-to-day actions, agent management workflows, reporting and analytics, and pain points. Platform walkthroughs conducted to observe actual task behavior.

Travel agents

Finding 1

Agents weren't exploring — they were trying to close decisions quickly

I expected agents to behave something like careful consumers — comparing options, narrowing gradually, weighing tradeoffs. The data suggested something different. Location (76.6%) and price (74.2%) dominated decision-making by a significant margin. Guest reviews came third at 54%. Everything else trailed considerably.

The contradiction was telling. Agents frequently requested more controls, more filters, richer information in open-ended responses — yet their actual decisions relied on a much smaller framework. What they asked for and what they used were different things.

Key insight

Agents weren't optimizing for exploration — they were optimizing for speed and certainty. Adding more filters risked increasing complexity without improving decisions.

Finding 2

Search friction was a trust problem, not a speed problem

Nearly 80% of agents identified searching for the right property as their most time-consuming task. My first interpretation was a speed problem. The open-ended responses complicated that picture — frustrations clustered around unavailable rooms appearing in results, inventory mismatches, pricing inconsistencies, and properties showing as available before revealing they weren't. These weren't speed issues. They were reliability issues.

Agents could search. They struggled to trust what they found. The fragmentation extended beyond the product too — only 23.4% used the platform's built-in quote feature. 36.3% shared options by screenshotting the compare screen and sending via WhatsApp. The platform was one node in a workflow agents had assembled themselves.

Key insight

Search confidence mattered more than search speed or volume. Solving for trust — not performance — was the more meaningful design problem.

Finding 3

Apartment decisions couldn't inherit hotel decision models

Survey data showed hotel and apartment decision factors overlapping — location, price, reviews appeared in both. But apartment decisions introduced additional considerations: size, facilities, amenities, guest policies. SME discussions revealed why the difference runs deeper than a longer checklist.

A hotel star rating is a cognitive shortcut — it bundles expectations about service quality, room standards, and security into a single number agents already know how to interpret. Apartments had no equivalent. Every attribute had to be evaluated manually, which meant agents had to assemble confidence from scratch for every property.

Hotel ratings act as cognitive shortcuts. Apartments lacked an equivalent trust framework — which meant the product had to create one rather than borrow from the hotel model.

GRNconnect admins

Finding 4

Admin work was far more operationally complex than the ecosystem view suggested

Study 1 established that admin workflows extended beyond the product into spreadsheets and manual coordination. The GRNconnect sessions added depth to that picture. Admin roles operated across three levels — super admin, admin, and sub-admin — each with distinct access rights, departmental scope, and responsibilities across operations, accounts, sales, and review teams.

Day-to-day work involved a continuous loop of registration approvals, agent assignments, credit limit management, KYC verification, performance monitoring, and follow-up. Platform walkthroughs showed how each of these tasks was currently handled — and how much of the actual work happened in Excel after downloading multiple reports that the platform couldn't consolidate.

"I wish I had one dashboard where I could see everything — active agents, top performers, failed bookings — without downloading 5 reports every morning."

Platform walkthroughs revealed the gap between what the platform supported and what admin work actually required — with significant effort happening outside the product entirely.

Key insight

The admin layer wasn't a simple management interface — it was a complex operational system. ApartWorld's infrastructure would need to support role-based access, consolidated reporting, and workflow continuity from the start, not as a later addition.

Finding 5

The tension between control and speed ran through both user groups

The most important cross-group pattern wasn't specific to agents or admins — it emerged from looking at both simultaneously. Admins needed control: verification gates, approval steps, visibility into agent behavior. Agents needed speed: fewer steps, faster results, less friction between search and booking.

These needs didn't just differ — they actively conflicted at specific moments. An approval gate that gave admins confidence was a delay that cost agents time. A credit limit check that protected the business was an interruption that broke an agent's flow with a waiting client. Neither user was wrong. The product had to navigate the tension between them deliberately, not accidentally resolve it in favor of one group.

Role-specific design wasn't just a UX preference — it was the only way to serve both users without systematically disadvantaging one of them.

How this shaped product direction

The agent findings challenged assumptions that had been shaping early design conversations — around filters, information density, and how apartment booking would work. The admin findings established what the operational infrastructure would need to support from day one.

Together they also surfaced a strategic decision: the trust gap in apartment decisions couldn't be solved by borrowing from the hotel model. This triggered a broader workshop with stakeholders, SMEs, and development leads — shifting the conversation from "how do we borrow hotel ratings?" to "how might ApartWorld create its own trust framework for apartments?" That led to a phased rating strategy built around guest-generated ratings first, a platform-specific AW Rating second, and transparency around how ratings are calculated third.

Reflection

Running two methods across two user groups in parallel created breadth that neither alone would have produced. The survey gave patterns at scale; the admin sessions gave depth and operational specificity. The most useful moments were where the two groups' findings converged — both were working around the product rather than inside it, both had built their own infrastructure to compensate for what the platform didn't support.

The main limitation on the agent side was the inability to follow up survey responses with interviews. Several findings — particularly around trust and the mental models agents used for apartment decisions — would have been stronger with direct conversation behind them. On the admin side, 3–5 sessions is enough to surface patterns but not enough to be confident about edge cases across different organizational structures.

I also used AI-assisted tools selectively to generate edge cases and challenge assumptions — treating outputs as prompts for better questions rather than as evidence. Every finding that influenced a product decision came from the research itself.

Understanding users meant understanding two very different kinds of pressure — agents optimizing for speed under client demand, admins managing operational complexity across a system they couldn't fully see. The product had to serve both, without accidentally resolving the tension between them in favor of one.
