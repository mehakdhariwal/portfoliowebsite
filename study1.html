Study 1 of 4

Understanding the ecosystem

Mapping stakeholders, competitive landscape, and hidden dependencies before designing anything

MethodsInterviews · Journey mapping · Competitive benchmarking · Ecosystem mapping
ParticipantsAdmin session · Agent walkthrough · 25+ platforms analyzed
ToolsMiro · FigJam · Excel · Teams

The starting point

When I joined the ApartWorld project, the team already had feature requirements and early assumptions about what needed to be built. The instinct was to start designing — booking flows, search interfaces, dashboards.

I pushed back on that. Two things were happening simultaneously that made me uncomfortable moving straight to screens.

First, different stakeholders described the same process in completely different ways. Sales talked about acquisition. Admins talked about onboarding and monitoring. Agents talked about closing bookings quickly. Operations talked about handling failures. This wasn't a difference of opinion — it was a signal that work was distributed across people in ways we didn't yet understand.

Second, we were entering a product category with established players and existing user expectations. Before deciding what ApartWorld should be, I wanted to understand what the category already looked like — what models existed, where they clustered, and what gaps they left open.

I ran both investigations in parallel: one looking inward at how work moved across the people who would use the product, and one looking outward at what the competitive landscape could tell us about where this product might credibly sit.

"Before designing interfaces, I needed to understand both how work happened inside the ecosystem and what the category already expected of a product like this."

Research questions

  • 1Who are the key actors in the ecosystem, and how do their workflows depend on each other?
  • 2Where does friction emerge — and how much of it happens outside the product entirely?
  • 3What models and patterns already exist in the competitive landscape?
  • 4What does the category currently leave underserved?
  • 5Which workflows should the product prioritize for MVP?

Approach

Two parallel investigations, each using the other as a pressure test. Internal findings were cross-checked against competitive patterns. Competitive gaps were validated against what users actually described.

Looking inward

Stakeholder interviews, workflow mapping, journey mapping, proto-persona development, pain-point synthesis, opportunity mapping

Looking outward

Structured analysis of 25+ platforms across business model, user groups, supply type, booking model, USP, and differentiating features

What the competitive landscape revealed

I cast wide — looking at large OTAs like Agoda and Expedia, niche players like Plum Guide, corporate-focused platforms like Apartool and SACO, and aggregators like HomeToGo. The goal wasn't feature comparison. It was pattern recognition — what models existed, what assumptions each platform made about its users, and where the category clustered.

Three structural patterns emerged quickly. The market had a clear B2B/B2C split, with very few platforms doing both well. Trust mechanisms were underdeveloped in the apartment category — hotels had star ratings as a cognitive shorthand, apartments didn't. And the agent and corporate booking layer was consistently underserved — where B2B features existed, they felt grafted onto consumer products rather than built from the ground up.

What the internal ecosystem revealed

At the same time, I was building understanding of the internal ecosystem. The picture that emerged was more complex than a booking product. ApartWorld involved over ten interdependent actor types — super admins, sub-admins, sales teams, operations teams, accounts teams, agency owners, agents, sub-agents, customers, and supplier APIs. Every booking touched multiple of these actors. Most of the friction happened at the handoffs between them.

ApartWorld was not a single-user product. Every action depended on multiple actors and handoffs — with critical dependencies on external systems and internal teams that users couldn't see.

Finding 1

Much of the work happened outside the product

The strongest early pattern wasn't about the interface at all. Critical work consistently happened outside the platform — in spreadsheets, WhatsApp threads, email chains, and manual tracking systems.

Admins regularly downloaded multiple reports, merged them in Excel, then made decisions from that synthesized view. Agents coordinated with customers over WhatsApp, shared quotes as PDFs, and tracked booking status in personal spreadsheets. The platform was a step in a larger workflow — not the center of it.

This recontextualized something from the competitive benchmarking. Many platforms I'd analyzed showed the same workaround behaviors in their reviews and support forums. The industry instinct had been to add more features inside the product. What I was seeing suggested the problem wasn't missing features — it was that products weren't designed around how work actually flowed.

Key insight

The product was not the workflow. Users had built their own infrastructure around the product — and any solution that ignored this would add to the problem rather than reduce it.

Finding 2

Admin work involved substantial invisible labor

Admin workflows looked simple from the outside: approve agents, assign permissions, monitor performance. Journey mapping told a different story.

To monitor performance, admins downloaded multiple reports, merged them manually in Excel, filtered the data themselves, identified underperforming agents, and then reached out individually by phone or email. This loop repeated daily. None of that work was visible inside the product.

"I wish I had one dashboard where I could see everything — active agents, top performers, failed bookings — without downloading 5 reports every morning."

The competitive benchmarking had flagged that advanced reporting and role-specific dashboards were rare even among mature competitors. This wasn't a niche need — it was a gap the category had largely ignored.

Key insight

The opportunity wasn't adding features. It was reducing the cognitive and operational effort the product was currently forcing onto users — effort the competitive landscape confirmed was an industry-wide problem, not just a platform-specific one.

Finding 3

Different users optimized for entirely different outcomes

Admins and agents both used the same platform but were optimizing for opposite things. Admins needed control — verification steps, approval gates, visibility into what agents were doing. Agents needed speed — fewer steps, faster results, less back-and-forth. These goals didn't just differ — they occasionally conflicted.

This tension was visible in the competitive landscape too. Platforms that had tried to serve both B2B operators and agents within the same experience had generally resolved it badly — either by giving agents a watered-down consumer experience or by burying operational controls in admin panels that were too slow to use.

Synthesis

Internal research and competitive benchmarking kept surfacing the same themes from different directions:

Manual coordination

Critical actions relied on emails, spreadsheets, and calls outside the product — a pattern confirmed by competitor reviews and support forums

Workflow fragmentation

Users switched across multiple systems to complete single tasks — an industry-wide condition, not an ApartWorld-specific problem

Underserved B2B layer

The agent and corporate booking experience was where the category had invested the least — making it the clearest strategic opening

How this shaped product direction

The two investigations converged on the same conclusion from different directions. The ecosystem work showed we weren't building a booking interface — we were building infrastructure to support an operational workflow. The competitive work showed the B2B agent layer was the category's most underserved audience.

Together, they made the case for starting there: build for agents and the B2B layer first, get that right, then expand toward direct consumer. That became an early strategic anchor for the project.

Reflection

One real limitation of this phase was limited direct access to users. I relied on a structured session with the platform admin, one agent walkthrough session, stakeholder knowledge, workflow documentation, and the competitive analysis to triangulate understanding — but couldn't observe people working in context. That would have surfaced things interviews missed.

The benchmarking had its own limitation: several platforms I attempted to analyze had limited public-facing information, and a few I'd identified as relevant couldn't be found or confirmed. The analysis was most valuable where I could go deep — and less reliable as a statistical picture of the whole category.

If I revisited this study, I'd push for contextual observation earlier, even informally. Watching someone merge Excel reports for 20 minutes tells you more than asking them about it.

Research changed the question from "What should the interface look like?" to "How does work actually happen — and what does the category tell us about where to focus first?"
